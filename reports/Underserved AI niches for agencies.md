# Target the trades the titans skipped

*Research report, October 2026. Not legal advice. How to read the labels: **[Verified]** means a primary source, such as Census data, a vendor's own pricing page or a court or regulator. **[Vendor claim]** means a company's marketing about itself. **[Estimate]** means a cost guide, a third-party blog or the author's own judgment. Treat anything not marked Verified as a working assumption to check.*

The best openings for a solo AI-automation agency in late 2026 are **owner-run specialty trades and event businesses that have no dominant industry software, or whose software has no built-in AI**. The strongest candidates are independent **garage door repair**, **fencing**, **concrete, flatwork and epoxy**, **septic and well**, **tree service**, **locksmiths** and **party, tent and event rentals**. Close behind are wedding venues, martial-arts and dance studios, and funeral homes. The niches every guru recommends (HVAC, plumbing, dental, salons, restaurants, auto dealers, property management, and now law firms on Clio) all have a "titan": a big platform the business already uses that now sells its own AI receptionist, often for $29 to $99 a month, and books straight onto the business's calendar. You would be competing with something the owner already owns. The demand is real everywhere. About **44% of inbound business calls are not answered by a person** (Invoca, July 2026), only **42% of callers leave a voicemail** (CallRail, 2025), and fewer than half of businesses even get the chance to qualify a lead quickly. So the question is not who needs AI, but **who needs it and can't get it from software they already pay for**. For your setup (GoHighLevel, websites plus AI chat, and a Lead Finder that searches OpenStreetMap and Chicago building permits), start with **garage door repair** this winter and add **fence and concrete contractors** in time for the spring 2027 season. Sell an inbound-first package, an AI receptionist plus missed-call text-back plus review requests, at about **$397–$497 a month plus a $497 setup fee**. Inbound-first matters because it keeps you clear of most of the legal risk in outbound AI calling.

## Four in ten calls go unanswered, and most callers never try again

The core pain is well documented, though many popular statistics are old or inflated. The largest recent dataset, Invoca's July 2026 benchmark of more than 70 million calls, found that **only 56% of inbound calls were answered by a person**. Home services ran about 52% and healthcare about 54% **[Verified headline; industry rates from Invoca's blog table]** ([Invoca](https://www.invoca.com/reports/the-invoca-lead-conversion-benchmarks-report-2026); [Invoca table](https://www.invoca.com/blog/table-test)). Invoca's separate home-services report puts **HVAC at only 34% answered and plumbing at 74%**, which shows how much answer rates vary by trade. That sub-trade breakdown comes from a secondary summary ([PPC Land](https://ppc.land/invoca-packages-ai-agents-as-google-bills-missed-lsa-calls-over-20-seconds/)). Invoca's customers skew toward larger advertisers, and CallRail's January 2025 small-business benchmark shows lower miss rates: **healthcare 32%, legal 28%, home services 14%** ([BusinessWire/CallRail](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20250114793850/en/CallRail-Releases-Report-Benchmarking-Marketing-Efforts-for-Small-Businesses)). The honest range for a sales pitch is "somewhere between one in seven and one in two calls, depending on the trade." Don't use the famous "62% of calls go unanswered" figure. It comes from a 2016 study of 85 businesses that counted voicemail as unanswered ([Alliance Virtual Offices](https://www.alliancevirtualoffices.com/virtual-office-blog/shocking-research-finds-small-businesses-miss-almost-half-of-incoming-calls/)).

What happens after a missed call matters more than the miss rate. **Only 42% of consumers leave a voicemail** when nobody answers, according to a 2025 CallRail survey of 1,000 US adults reported in a secondary summary ([summary of CallRail](https://answeringservicecare.com/blog/callers-leave-no-voicemail/)). That is why a text sent back within a minute is worth selling even on its own. On speed, the most rigorous study is still Harvard Business Review's 2011 audit of 2,241 US companies. **Firms that responded within an hour were nearly 7 times as likely to qualify a lead**, and **23% of firms never responded at all** ([Venture Harbour summary of HBR](https://ventureharbour.com/how-fast-do-marketing-leads-turn-cold-and-how-to-stop-it-happening/)). The "21 times more likely at 5 minutes" figure comes from a 2007 InsideSales study, not from MIT, so treat it as directional ([original PDF](https://www.mortech.com/hs-fs/hub/25649/file-13535879-pdf/docs/mit_study.pdf)). There is also a hard-cash argument for any client running Google Local Services Ads: **Google bills for missed calls over 20 seconds**, so an unanswered phone means paying for leads that never get answered ([PPC Land](https://ppc.land/invoca-packages-ai-agents-as-google-bills-missed-lsa-calls-over-20-seconds/)).

The adoption gap shows where the money is. In the Census Bureau's national survey, only **about 20% of US businesses used AI as of May 2026**. Use was lowest at firms with **4 or fewer employees** and in **retail (about 14%)**, while information firms were at 39.7% **[Verified]** ([Census](https://census.gov/library/stories/2026/05/ai-use-businesses.html)). A secondary source reports that construction rose to about 15% by September 2026 ([startbusinessbystate](https://startbusinessbystate.com/small-business-ai-use-by-state/)). Owners also say why they haven't adopted. In Goldman Sachs' 2026 small-business survey, the top barriers were **lack of expertise (49%) and difficulty choosing tools (48%)** ([Goldman Sachs](https://www.goldmansachs.com/community-impact/10000-small-businesses-voices/insights/ai-presents-a-major-opportunity-for-small-businesses)). A done-for-you agency solves exactly those two problems. Customers will accept AI on the condition that they can still reach a person: **61% are comfortable with AI customer service** (Adobe, November 2025), and **87% say an option to reach a human is essential** (Gartner, 2026) ([Adobe](https://www.adobe.com/express/learn/blog/future-of-conversational-marketing); [Gartner via Macha](https://www.getmacha.com/blog/customer-trust-ai-customer-service-statistics)). Every package you sell should include a path to a callback or a human.

## Inbound-first keeps you out of TCPA court

The single most important legal fact is this: **calls made with an AI voice count as "artificial or prerecorded voice" calls under the TCPA** (FCC ruling 24-17, Feb 2024). That means outbound AI calls need prior express consent, and **written** consent for marketing. Damages run **$500–$1,500 per call or text** ([Kelley Drye](https://www.kelleydrye.com/viewpoints/blogs/ad-law-access/fcc-proposes-new-tcpa-rules-for-ai-calls-and-texts); [FCC](https://www.fcc.gov/consumers/guides/stop-unwanted-robocalls-and-texts)). AI that answers inbound calls is far lower risk. The FCC's proposed AI rules explicitly avoid burdening inbound customer-service technology ([Wiley](https://wiley.law/alert-FCC-Proposes-New-Rules-for-AI-Generated-Calls-and-Texts)), but whether they have been finalized is unconfirmed as of October 2026. The 2024 "one-to-one consent" rule was **struck down in January 2025** and later deleted ([MoFo](https://www.mofo.com/resources/insights/250130-eleventh-circuit-vacates-fcc-s-tcpa-one-to-one-consent-rule)). Rules on revoking consent are in effect, however: you must honor an opt-out made by **any reasonable means within 10 business days**. A broader "revoke-all" piece is delayed to **January 31, 2027**, though that comes from secondary sources ([ActiveProspect](https://activeprospect.com/blog/fcc-tcpa/)).

Because you work out of Chicago, the recording rules matter on day one. **Illinois is listed among the states that require every party's consent to record a call**, along with California, Florida, Pennsylvania, Washington and others. Treat that list as background knowledge and verify it per state ([Justia](https://www.justia.com/50-state-surveys/recording-phone-calls-and-conversations/)). Plaintiffs in California are also suing AI vendors as third-party "eavesdroppers," and no court has settled that theory yet ([Fisher Phillips](https://www.fisherphillips.com/en/insights/insights/new-class-action-targets-healthcare-ai-recordings)). On AI disclosure, laws requiring a chatbot to say it's AI are in force in **California, Utah, New York and Maine**. **Colorado, Connecticut, Oregon and Washington** follow on **January 1, 2027**, according to a secondary roll-up not checked against the statutes ([Entagl](https://www.entagl.com/blog/us-ai-chatbot-disclosure-laws-2026)). A federal executive order (EO 14365) attacks state AI laws, but **Congress has not passed preemption**, so state laws still apply ([McDermott](https://mwe.com/insights/white-house-eo-moves-to-restrict-state-ai-legislation)).

## A garage-door-first 90-day launch built on permit data

**Start with garage door repair now, and add fence and concrete contractors in February.** The reasoning is part evidence, part judgment. Garage door work is emergency, phone-first and high-ticket. No garage-door software with built-in AI turned up, and the industry code shows the highest payroll per business of any open niche (about **$1.68M**, though that code also includes elevator contractors). Its demand is also less seasonal than outdoor trades. Fencing and concrete are your best fit for **Chicago building-permit data**, since permits show which contractors are active and how many jobs they pull. The research did not check which Chicago permit types cover fences and flatwork, so confirm that in your data first. Use OpenStreetMap for locksmiths and storefront trades as a secondary source. OSM coverage of trade businesses is uneven, an untested inference, so cross-check against Google Business listings. One more caution: phone them yourself, not with an AI dialer, to stay inside the TCPA rules above.

## Conclusion

The common advice to "pick a high-ticket niche" now has a second test that matters as much as job value: **does the business's own software already answer the phone?** In 2026 that test rules out most of the obvious markets, including HVAC, dental, salons, restaurants, pools, pest control and, since August, law firms on Clio. The openings that remain are unglamorous, fragmented, owner-run trades and event businesses that run on QuickBooks, Google Voice and paper, plus a few niches like towing and self-storage where the titan has announced AI but not shipped it. Those windows will close, so speed matters more than perfecting the product.

The research also leaves real gaps. No independent study measures missed calls for garage door, fencing or locksmith shops; no survey measures what owners will pay; and agency pricing data comes almost entirely from vendors selling to agencies. That makes your first 30 days of secret-shopper calls and founding-client results more valuable than any statistic in this report, because they become **local, verified proof** that national vendors can't fake. The lasting advantage for a solo agency is not the AI. It is being the person in Chicago who knows how garage door and fence companies actually lose jobs, and can prove they've stopped losing them.
