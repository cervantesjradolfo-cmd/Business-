# Win the niches AI agencies keep skipping

The best openings for a small web/AI agency are in **hands-on, local businesses that still answer the phone and write quotes by hand**. That means construction trades, event and party rental, and remodeling-type contractors. It does not mean the dentists, real estate agents and HVAC shops that every AI course tells beginners to chase. The most reliable government and bank data show construction, food service, transportation and similar "physical work" sectors using AI at **under 10%**, compared with roughly 30-40% for office-type businesses. These sectors also miss a large share of their calls and leads. Where a single job is worth thousands of dollars, one recovered customer a month pays for an agency's whole fee. The niches that are **both untouched and high-ticket** are commercial wall-and-ceiling subcontractors (drywall, framing, acoustic ceilings), larger tent/table/wedding rental companies and wedding venues, and residential remodelers and similar trades below the roofing/HVAC tier. Your two areas of hands-on experience land almost exactly on that overlap. The biggest caveat is the evidence itself. Nearly every missed-call statistic comes from companies selling call-answering software, and the "crowded vs. open" ratings rest on what the researchers could find in search results, not on any count of agencies. Treat this report as a well-grounded starting map. Before you build a pitch around any number, check it against your prospect's own call logs.

## Hands-on local businesses use AI at a quarter the rate of office businesses

The two strongest data sources agree closely. The US Census Bureau's business survey covers a huge, nationally representative sample and asks whether a firm used AI in the last two weeks. It found **about 18-20% of US businesses using AI in late 2025 to May 2026**. Information firms were at 38-40%, professional services at 34% and finance at 30-34% ([Census working paper, Apr 2026](https://www2.census.gov/library/working-papers/2026/adrm/ces/CES-WP-26-25.pdf); [Census, May 2026](https://www.census.gov/library/stories/2026/05/ai-use-businesses.html)). The Minneapolis Fed reads the same survey and puts **agriculture, transportation, accommodation and food services, and construction each under 10%** ([Minneapolis Fed, May 2026](https://www.minneapolisfed.org/article/2026/ai-adoption-in-business-grows-steadily-but-unevenly)). The Federal Reserve Board puts restaurants and hotels at **8%** ([Federal Reserve, Apr 2026](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/monitoring-ai-adoption-in-the-u-s-economy-20260403.html)). JPMorganChase looked at what 4.6 million small businesses actually paid for in their bank accounts. It found **17.7% had ever paid for an AI tool by end of 2025**, with **construction at 8.9% and transportation at 5.4%**, against 39% for information businesses ([JPMorganChase Institute](https://www.jpmorganchase.com/institute/all-topics/business-growth-and-entrepreneurship/understanding-ai-use-by-small-businesses)). Two very different measuring methods produced the same answer and the same ranking, which makes this the most trustworthy finding in the research.

The gap is widening. In 2023-24 the Census survey showed construction and agriculture at 1.4% and information at 18.1%, about a 17-point spread ([Census working paper, 2024](https://www.census.gov/hfp/btos/downloads/CES-WP-24-16.pdf)). By 2025 the JPMorganChase figures showed roughly a 30-point spread. The same 2024 Census paper pointed out that **more than 6 million US firms are small businesses in construction, "other services" (salons, dry cleaners, auto repair) and food service, all low-adoption sectors**. The smallest firms (1-4 employees) expect almost no change in their own AI use over the next six months, 18% rising to 21% ([Census, Apr 2026](https://www2.census.gov/library/working-papers/2026/adrm/ces/CES-WP-26-25.pdf)). The researchers concluded that these owners will not adopt AI on their own and will need someone to set it up for them. That is a judgment, but it fits the data.

You will see much higher numbers elsewhere. The US Chamber of Commerce reports 58% of small businesses "using AI", with construction at 47% ([IPWatchdog summary](https://ipwatchdog.com/2025/08/18/us-chamber-report-small-businesses-rapidly-adopting-ai-despite-regulatory-concerns/)). Thryv, a software vendor, reports 55% ([Thryv](https://assets.thryv.com/prod/media/pdfs/guides/AI-and-Small-Business-Adoption-2025-FINAL.pdf)). Housecall Pro reports 48% of trades pros among its mostly-customer sample ([Housecall Pro](https://www.housecallpro.com/resources/ai-in-the-trades/)). These surveys use self-selected panels or the vendor's own customers, and they count "software I already use that now has AI in it". They tell you that tech-savvy owners are experimenting. They do not show that the typical plumber or rental company has an AI system working for them.

What these businesses do with AI matters even more than whether they use it. Most small-business AI use is the owner typing into ChatGPT or Canva for writing and marketing. In the NFIB's member survey, **only 9% of AI users applied it to customer service** ([NFIB 2025](https://www.nfib.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/2025-NFIB-Technology-Survey.pdf)). Done-for-you systems that face the customer are still rare: AI receptionist, automatic booking, instant lead follow-up, quoting, review requests. The Census survey lists customer service among the uses businesses expect to grow fastest ([Census, Apr 2026](https://www2.census.gov/library/working-papers/2026/adrm/ces/CES-WP-26-25.pdf)). When trades firms do adopt AI, customer follow-up (52%) and quoting (51%) top their list ([Housecall Pro](https://www.housecallpro.com/resources/ai-in-the-trades/)). Demand is forming, and few providers are serving it yet.

The main obstacle is not price. Among businesses with no AI plans, **65% say AI is "not applicable to my business"**, while only about 4% called it too expensive in the earlier Census round ([Census, Apr 2026](https://www2.census.gov/library/working-papers/2026/adrm/ces/CES-WP-26-25.pdf); [Census, 2024](https://www.census.gov/hfp/btos/downloads/CES-WP-24-16.pdf)). In the NFIB survey, 42% of owners were "not at all familiar" with AI ([NFIB](https://www.nfib.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/2025-NFIB-Technology-Survey.pdf)). So in practice, don't sell "AI". Sell "you'll stop losing the calls you miss while you're on a job" and "you'll get more 5-star reviews".

## Missed calls and slow replies are the pain worth selling against

Every vertical studied loses a meaningful share of customers at first contact. Almost all of this data comes from call-tracking and answering companies, which benefit when miss rates look high. Each company also defines "missed" differently, so the numbers below show the size of the problem rather than precise benchmarks.

| Niche | Share of calls missed or unanswered | Source and caveat |
|---|---|---|
| Home services / trades | 14% missed (CallRail); only 52% answered by a person (Invoca 2026) | Both vendors; very different definitions ([CallRail](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20250114793850/en/CallRail-Releases-Report-Benchmarking-Marketing-Efforts-for-Small-Businesses); [Invoca](https://invoca.com/reports/the-invoca-home-services-lead-conversion-benchmarks-report-2026)) |
| Healthcare / dental | 32% (CallRail, highest of any industry); 20-38% in dental vendor data | Vendor ([CallRail](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20250114793850/en/CallRail-Releases-Report-Benchmarking-Marketing-Efforts-for-Small-Businesses); [Peerlogic](https://www.peerlogic.com/post/peerlogic-dso-case-study)) |
| Law firms | 28% (CallRail); in Clio's secret-shopper test, 48% didn't answer or call back and 67% never answered an email | Clio is a vendor but its report is the industry standard ([Above the Law](https://abovethelaw.com/2024/10/law-firms-failing-at-the-single-most-basic-lawyering-task/)) |
| Salons / spas | 37%, with 82% of misses during business hours | Salon software vendor ([Zenoti](https://www.zenoti.com/thecheckin/ai-receptionist-roi)) |
| Auto service | 10-21% | Older, multi-location data from a vendor ([Marchex](https://www.marchex.com/up-to-21-percent-of-automotive-service-customer-calls-go-unanswered-creating-significant-revenue-leakage/)) |
| Real estate | 9% | Vendor ([CallRail](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20250114793850/en/CallRail-Releases-Report-Benchmarking-Marketing-Efforts-for-Small-Businesses)) |

Two findings matter more for your pitch than any single miss rate. First, **Invoca found 55% of home-services businesses never asked the caller to book**. The failure is not only unanswered phones but also weak follow-through on the calls that are answered ([Invoca](https://invoca.com/reports/the-invoca-home-services-lead-conversion-benchmarks-report-2026)). Second, Google now bills trades for missed Local Services Ads calls over 20 seconds ([PPC Land](https://ppc.land/invoca-packages-ai-agents-as-google-bills-missed-lsa-calls-over-20-seconds/)). A missed call is no longer just a lost job. The contractor is paying for it.

The "speed to lead" research is old but consistent. A 2007 study by MIT researchers and InsideSales (a vendor) found that contacting a web lead within 5 minutes made reaching the person **100 times more likely** than waiting 30 minutes ([study summary](https://www.mortech.com/hs-fs/hub/25649/file-13535879-pdf/docs/mit_study.pdf)). A 2011 Harvard Business Review audit of 2,241 US companies found **23% never responded to a web inquiry at all** ([BYU record of the HBR study](https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/facpub/9711)). Nothing since then shows small businesses improving. A 2024 mystery-shop of 92 franchise brands found only half followed up on a web form ([Franchising.com](https://www.franchising.com/articles/mystery_shopping_24_researchers_investigate_first_contact_with_potential_fr.html)). An owner standing on a ladder or under a bounce house cannot reply in five minutes, which is the core argument for an instant automatic text, chat or AI callback.

Be careful with the most-quoted statistic in this industry, "62% of small-business calls go unanswered". It comes from a single 411 Locals study of **just 85 businesses**. The research notes disagree on whether it dates from 2016 or 2024, and it is often presented as recent ([OnCrew](https://oncrew.ai/resources/missed-call-statistics); [Alliance Virtual Offices](https://www.alliancevirtualoffices.com/virtual-office-blog/shocking-research-finds-small-businesses-miss-almost-half-of-incoming-calls/)). The equally popular "$126K a year lost to missed calls" figure has no clear method behind it. One source states plainly that **no published study measures the real dollar loss per missed call** for something like an HVAC shop ([ainora.lt](https://ainora.lt/blog/hvac-service-call-statistics-2026)). The honest pitch multiplies the prospect's own missed calls by their booking rate and their average job value.

## The untouched niches: where neither AI tools nor agencies have arrived

To count as "untouched", a niche needs low AI use plus little competition from agencies and from the industry software owners already pay for. That second test matters a lot now. The big software platforms in several niches have built AI receptionists into the product. **ServiceTitan** (HVAC, plumbing) launched AI voice agents that book straight onto the dispatch board ([ServiceTitan](https://www.servicetitan.com/blog/pantheon-2025-pro-products-automation)). **Jobber** launched an AI Receptionist that answers calls and texts 24/7 ([PM Magazine](https://www.pmmag.com/articles/106754-jobber-launches-ai-powered-receptionist)). **Weave**, the dental phone system the American Dental Association now endorses, bought an AI receptionist company ([ADA News](https://adanews.ada.org/ada-news/2026/march/ada-member-advantage-endorses-weave-as-patient-engagement-platform/)). **Zenoti** (salons, med spas) and **Ylopo** (real estate) do the same ([Zenoti](https://www.zenoti.com/thecheckin/zenoti-vs-boulevard); [Ylopo](https://www.ylopo.com/ylopo-timeline)). Pitch a standalone AI receptionist to an HVAC shop on ServiceTitan and you'll hear "my software already does that." On top of that, AI-agency courses keep steering beginners into home services, dental, medical and real estate ([Ciela](https://ciela.ai/blogs/best-ai-automation-agency-niche-for-beginners)). Community discussion now calls generic dental and real-estate pitches "oversaturated" ([Ciela summary of Reddit](https://ciela.ai/blogs/is-ai-agency-market-saturated-reddit)). That source is itself a vendor summarizing secondhand.

The table below combines the research team's ratings. **The crowding column is the researchers' judgment.** It is based on how many niche-specific AI vendors and agency offers turned up in searches. No one has counted agencies per niche.

|---|---|---|---|

Low AI adoption in a sector does not mean nobody is selling into it. Construction as a whole is under 10%, yet roofing and HVAC are among the most-pitched niches in the agency world. The real test is whether the niche's own software already does the job. By that test, **event rental and commercial specialty subcontracting are the clearest open lanes**. In both, the work is still mostly manual: rental quotes are built from rules and chased by hand, and "most specialty subcontractors are still doing takeoffs the same way they did 20 years ago" ([AlphaBOLD](https://www.alphabold.com/webinars/boldwebinar-ai-assisted-takeoff-automation/), a vendor webinar). Trade press says AI in the trades is "moving past the receptionist job" toward deeper workflow help ([ServiceMag](https://servicemag.org/news/ai-workflow-agents-trades-beyond-receptionist)). As answering phones becomes a commodity, quoting, estimating and follow-up become the better thing to sell.

## The high-ticket niches: where one saved customer pays the whole bill

A niche is "high ticket" when each job is worth enough that recovering a single customer covers your fee. Here is what a typical job or client is worth, from the best available cost guides:

| Value tier | Niches and typical value |
|---|---|
| **Very high ($10K+ per win)** | Pool builds ~$66K typical ([Angi](https://www.angi.com/articles/how-much-does-it-cost-install-pool.htm)); full kitchen remodel $65K-$130K+ ([Angi](https://www.angi.com/articles/10-most-popular-home-remodeling-projects-and-how-much-they-cost.htm)); solar ~$31K ([EnergySage](https://www.energysage.com/local-data/solar-panel-cost/)); home care aide ~$78K/yr per full-time client ([Home Health Care News](https://homehealthcarenews.com/2025/03/cost-of-home-based-care-services-spiked-in-2024/)); private school ~$15K/yr ([DC Schools](https://dcschools.com/statistics/us-private-school-tuition-2026)); wedding venue ~$12,900 ([The Knot](https://www.theknot.com/content/average-cost-reception-venue)); personal-injury law at ~33% of the settlement ([Mighty](https://www.mighty.com/blog/standard-personal-injury-fees)); commercial subcontracts (no published average; the researchers estimate $50K to millions) |
| **High ($3K-$15K)** | Roof replacement ~$9,500 ([Porter](https://callporter.com/blog/renovation-costs/)); HVAC replacement ~$7,500 ([Angi](https://www.angi.com/articles/insider-s-price-guide-new-heating-and-cooling-system.htm)); hardscaping ~$9,000 ([Angi](https://www.angi.com/articles/what-rule-thumb-labor-cost-vs-material-cost-hardscape-projects.htm)); collision repair $4,818 ([CCC](https://www.cccis.com/news-and-insights/posts/ccc-crash-course-2026-report-finds-higher-severity-and-record-total-loss-frequency)); cross-country move $4,572 ([HomeAdvisor](https://www.homeadvisor.com/cost/storage-and-organization/long-distance-move/)); divorce ~$11,300 ([Motley Fool, citing Martindale-Nolo 2019](https://www.fool.com/money/research/average-cost-of-divorce/)); dental implants and orthodontics ~$2K-$6K+ ([CareCredit](https://www.carecredit.com/well-u/health-wellness/dental-implants-cost-dental-implants-financing/)) |
| **Lower ticket, repeat value** | Med spa visit ~$216 median ([Zenoti](https://www.zenoti.com/en-uk/thecheckin/medspa-trends-2026)); vet visit ~$200 ([AVMA](https://www.avma.org/news/evolving-pet-owner-economics-what-data-reveal-veterinary-teams)); bounce-house rental $75-$500/day ([ThePricer](https://www.thepricer.org/cost-to-rent-a-bounce-house/)); wedding-scale event rentals ~$2,000 ([Complete Weddings, citing The Knot](https://completewedo.com/blog/what-are-real-couples-spending-on-weddings/)) |

These businesses also pay a lot for each lead, which raises the cost of every lead they drop. On Google search ads, the average cost per lead is about **$67 across all industries**. It is **$132 for law firms, $103 for real estate, $91 for home improvement and $73 for dentists**, but only about $30 for auto repair ([LocaliQ 2026 benchmarks](https://localiq.com/blog/search-advertising-benchmarks/)). Residential solar spends roughly $0.60-$0.84 per watt to win a customer, several thousand dollars per sale on a typical system ([Wood Mackenzie](https://www.woodmac.com/news/opinion/us-residential-solar-customer-acquisition-costs-set-to-spike-40-in-2026-before-gradual-decline/)).

Now compare what you can charge. Off-the-shelf AI receptionists cost about **$50-$300 a month** ([TRTC pricing roundup](https://trtc.io/blog/details/ai-receptionist-pricing-2026)). Agencies building custom AI voice and follow-up systems commonly charge **$1,000-$5,000 to set up plus $300-$2,000 a month** ([Ciela](https://ciela.ai/blogs/how-much-to-charge-for-ai-voice-agent), an agency-tool vendor). For comparison, a human receptionist earns a median $18.27 an hour ([BLS](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/office-and-administrative-support/receptionists.htm)). Simple arithmetic does the rest: **at $500-$1,500 a month, the system pays for itself if it saves one $3,000+ job a month.** In pools, remodeling, home care or personal-injury law, one saved client covers months or years of fees. That arithmetic also explains why a cheap $79 tool is your real competition in low-ticket niches. To justify a markup, you have to provide what the cheap tools don't: setup wired into the client's calendar and software, follow-up sequences, quoting help, and reporting.

High ticket alone doesn't make a good fit. A niche also needs inbound inquiries that reward speed. The researchers sorted niches this way, and the tiers are their judgment, not a published ranking. The best fits have big tickets **and** urgent or comparison-shopping callers: roofing, HVAC, personal-injury law, dental implants and orthodontics, plastic surgery, collision repair and home care. Next come very large tickets with slower, weeks-long decisions, where AI is better at booking consultations and nurturing leads than at answering phones: solar, pools, remodeling, hardscaping, family law, moving and wedding venues. **Commercial subcontractors, real estate, mortgage and insurance are "relationship" niches.** Their work comes through bids and referrals, not Google searches, so the value is in follow-up and back-office help rather than a receptionist.

## Where untouched meets high ticket, and where your experience fits

Put the two lists side by side. Most high-ticket, fast-response niches (roofing, HVAC, dental, personal-injury law, med spas) are exactly where agencies and software vendors already crowd. The niches that are **both** untouched and high-ticket form a short list. Commercial wall-and-ceiling subcontractors fit (very large contracts, low AI use, almost no agency attention), but they need an operations sale rather than a marketing one. Tent/table/wedding-scale event rental companies and wedding venues fit (thousands per booking, little customer-facing AI in their software). Residential remodelers, insulation, basement-finishing and hardscape contractors probably fit, since construction AI use is under 10% and they get less specialist-agency attention than roofers. That last call is the researchers' judgment, because nobody has counted agencies per niche. **Home care agencies** have strong numbers: ~$78K a year per client, and stressed families calling for help. The research did not check how crowded they are, so treat them as a promising unknown. Pool builders have big tickets, but nobody researched competition for builders specifically, only for pool service.

Your experience lines up with this overlap unusually well.

**Party and bounce-house rentals.** You already know the software (InflatableOffice, Goodshuffle Pro, Booqable), the seasonal rush, and the evening and weekend inquiry pattern. None of those platforms answers customers with AI. InflatableOffice and Goodshuffle showed no AI features at all ([Software Advice](https://www.softwareadvice.com/rental/inflatableoffice-profile/); [Capterra](https://www.capterra.com/p/167364/Goodshuffle-Pro/)). One rental business listed for sale had built its own "AI-assisted phone system" on top of Goodshuffle, a sign that operators are trying to fill the gap themselves ([BizQuest](https://www.bizquest.com/business-for-sale/established-event-rental-company-with-proven-systems/BW2432541/)). The market is growing, with the American Rental Association forecasting **+9.5% for party and event rental in 2026 to $6.2B** ([Rental Equipment Register](https://www.rermag.com/news-analysis/headline-news/article/55400064/aras-latest-quarterly-forecast-projects-continuing-growth-through-2028)). Two cautions apply. First, a consultancy is already marketing an "AI front desk" to party rental companies on exactly these platforms ([Stormy](https://stormy.ai/consult/party-rental/front-desk/inflatableoffice)), so the lane is open but no longer empty. Second, bounce-house-only operators are small: $150-$300 per party ([Reservety](https://reservety.com/guides/bounce-house/bounce-house-business-plan.html), a vendor) and around $100K in yearly revenue for one listed business ([BizQuest](https://images.bizquest.com/business-for-sale/profitable-bounce-house-biz-100k-revenue/BW2402583)). The researchers' judgment is to **move up-market to tent, table and wedding rental companies**, whose $2,000+ quote-based orders can carry a real retainer. Wedding venues (~$12,900 per booking) are the natural next step from there.

**Construction subcontracting.** Your Chicago drywall/framing/acoustic-ceiling client gives you a working example in a niche almost no marketing agency serves. These firms don't win work from Google. They win it from general contractors' bid invitations, mostly through Autodesk's BuildingConnected ([Autodesk](https://www.autodesk.com/blogs/construction/streamline-bid-management/)). Their real problems are sorting bid invitations, doing takeoffs quickly, and finding workers. **92% of construction firms struggle to fill jobs** ([NCCER/AGC](https://www.nccer.org/media/2025/10/AGC-NCCER_Workforce-Survey_infographic2025-Final.pdf)), and **over 90% of wall-and-ceiling contractors report shortages of both crews and field managers** ([Walls & Ceilings, AWCI report](https://www.wconline.com/articles/97837-awci-unveils-wall-and-ceiling-industry-trends-report)). The market is large and fragmented, with roughly 18,000-19,500 employer firms in drywall and insulation ([VerticalIQ](https://verticaliq.com/?p=3588)). The downsides are real. Over 70% of AWCI members expect a flat or declining market ([Walls & Ceilings](https://www.wconline.com/articles/97837-awci-unveils-wall-and-ceiling-industry-trends-report)), owners worry about data accuracy and security ([Roads & Bridges](https://roadsbridges.com/technology/news/55337001/report-most-contractors-expect-ai-to-change-how-projects-get-built)), and sales take longer. AI takeoff software such as Togal now targets drywall directly, so don't try to build a takeoff tool. Build the workflow around the tools these firms already use.

**General local-business web design.** This gives you the base layer every niche needs: a fast site, online booking, review collection. It is also how you get in the door. Lead with a website or booking fix, then add the AI follow-up once you've proven yourself.

## The shortlist, and what to sell each niche

The ranking below is the researchers' judgment. It weighs low competition, ticket size, fit with your experience and ease of reaching owners.

| Rank | Niche | Category | What to sell | Why |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Tent, table and wedding-scale event rental companies** | Untouched + mid/high ticket | Website with live availability and booking tied to their rental software; 24/7 AI chat answering "is it available on my date, will it fit, what's the weather policy"; automatic quote follow-up by text; abandoned-cart recovery; post-event review requests | Closest to your experience; software has no customer-facing AI; $2K+ orders; spring rush overwhelms owners |
| 2 | **Commercial wall-and-ceiling subcontractors** (drywall, framing, acoustic ceilings, insulation) | Untouched + very high ticket | An "AI bid desk": sorting and summarizing incoming bid invites and addenda, drafting scope and exclusion letters, follow-up reminders to general contractors after bids, a hiring page and applicant-screening funnel, and a credibility website for GCs and recruits | Live client to point to; almost no agency competition; contracts often worth six figures or more (researchers' estimate). Expect slow sales and cautious owners |
| 3 | **Residential remodelers, basement finishers, insulation, hardscape** (Chicago area first) | Mostly untouched + high ticket | Instant text-back on missed calls and web forms, an AI receptionist that books estimate visits, a photo-based quote intake form, review automation | Construction sits under 10% AI use; $9K-$100K jobs; less crowded than roofing (judgment); uses your trades vocabulary |
| 4 | **Wedding and event venues** | Partly untouched + high ticket | AI inquiry response on date, price and capacity; tour booking; follow-up sequences over the long decision window | ~$12,900 per booking; evening and weekend inquiries; natural step up from rentals |
| 5 | **Home care agencies** (stretch pick) | High ticket; crowding unknown | Always-on answering for family inquiries, fast callback, consultation booking, caregiver recruiting funnel | ~$78K/yr per client; competition not researched, so test with a few calls before committing |

Skip as a primary target, or go in only with a sharp angle: dental, real estate agents, HVAC and plumbing shops on ServiceTitan or Jobber, med spas on Zenoti, and roofing. In each, built-in software or funded startups already do the core job, and owners are tired of agency pitches. Keep **bounce-house-only operators** as a low-priced, packaged side product, such as a fixed-price "booking site + AI chat + reviews" bundle. Their tickets are too small for custom retainers.

Two selling principles come straight from the data. First, **sell the result, not the technology**. Two-thirds of owners who aren't using AI think it doesn't apply to them, so the words "AI agent" mean little to them. "You'll answer every inquiry within a minute, even at 9 pm on a Saturday" lands. Second, **build ROI from the prospect's own numbers**. Pull their call log or form submissions for a month, count the misses, and multiply by their own close rate and average job. The industry statistics are too vendor-shaped to carry a pitch alone.

## How much of this to trust

The firmest ground is the adoption ranking. The Census Bureau and JPMorganChase bank data independently agree that physical, local-service sectors sit under 10% and office sectors at 30-40%. Ticket sizes from Angi, The Knot, CCC, Genworth and similar cost guides are reasonable averages, though local prices vary widely. Everything about missed calls, lost revenue per call, online-booking demand and AI-receptionist ROI comes from **companies that sell answering or AI products**. Several key reports (Invoca, CallRail, Clio) were read only through press releases or secondhand coverage. The classic speed-to-lead studies are 15-19 years old and co-produced by a sales-software vendor. No independent study of AI-receptionist ROI was found at all.

The "crowded vs. untouched" ratings are the researchers' judgment. They are based on how many niche-specific vendors and agency offers showed up in searches, plus secondhand summaries of agency community chatter. The ratings for septic and towing rest on finding *nothing*, which is weak evidence. The event-rental and subcontractor findings are better supported, because the researchers confirmed what the main software platforms do and don't offer. Industry size figures for both niches conflict between IBISWorld editions and between IBISWorld and the rental association, because they define the industry differently. No source gave typical drywall subcontract values, bid win rates, or AI adoption specific to event rental, venues or individual trades. Expect the open lanes to narrow: Booqable added its first AI in mid-2026, and an AI consultancy is already targeting party rentals.

## Conclusion

The opportunity is not where the AI-agency crowd is looking. Beginners get sent to dentists and HVAC shops because those owners obviously lose money on missed calls, and that same logic drew in software giants and funded startups first. The better ground is the businesses that the data shows are furthest behind and that the big platforms haven't reached: event rental and specialty construction. You already have proof-of-work in both. And as answering phones becomes a $79-a-month commodity, the defensible product is moving away from the receptionist and toward work that requires knowing the trade: building a rental quote, sorting a bid invitation, chasing a remodel estimate. That favors an agency that has actually built rental sites and served a drywall sub.

The practical next step is to test this cheaply before betting on it. The window looks open for perhaps a year or two in these niches, but the evidence on competition is thin. Pitch five tent/event rental companies and five wall-and-ceiling subs, using your existing work as the case study. Measure the misses in each prospect's own call and email logs, and let those response rates, rather than any industry statistic, decide which lane gets your full focus.
